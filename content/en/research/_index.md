<div class="paper-title">“Who Hasn’t Given Birth?” Counter-Narratives of Childbirth Injury on Chinese Social Media</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <img src="/images/counter_narratives.png">
  <div class="research-content">
    <p><strong>AEJMC 2026</strong> · New Orleans, LA · Aug 2026 · <a href="/files/counter-narratives.pdf">📊 View Poster</a></p>
<p>🏆 <em>Third Top Student Paper Award</em></p>
    <p>Childbirth injury is common among women who give birth, yet it remains largely absent from public discourse and is often normalized as part of maternal endurance. Drawing on counter-narrative theory and thematic and narrative analysis of Douban discussions, this study identifies four ways women challenge this normalization: naming bodily harm, exposing maternal erasure, accounting for unequal reproductive costs, and repairing intergenerational silence. The study highlights embodied experience as a positioning resource and epistemic repair as a counter-narrative practice that helps restore the speakability of childbirth injury.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
    <p><strong>NCA 2026</strong> · New Orleans, LA · Nov 2026 · <em>Accepted</em>
    <p>This study examines public debate following China’s approval of the nine-valent HPV vaccine for males in 2025. Combining topic modeling with qualitative frame analysis, it identifies competing interpretations of male HPV vaccination centered on commercial interests, gender inequality, sexual morality, policy inconsistency, and institutional distrust. The findings highlight how broader social grievances can shape vaccine skepticism beyond individual-level health concerns.</p>

<div class="paper-title">Just Giving Birth? Lay Knowledge Production around Childbirth Injury in Chinese Online Communities</div>

<div class="research-paper">

  <div class="research-content">

  <p><strong>NCA 2026</strong> · New Orleans, LA · Nov 2026 · <em>Accepted</em>

  <p>This study examines how women in Douban communities collectively produce lay knowledge about childbirth injury. Drawing on Haraway’s situated knowledges, it focuses on how differently positioned participants negotiate experience, compare bodily knowledge, and build shared understandings of reproductive injury. The study argues that collective knowledge in online communities emerges through the interaction of partial and situated perspectives, while also remaining shaped by social limits on what can be articulated and recognized.</p>

<!-- Project 1: Mum Jobs -->

<div class="paper-title">What Problem Do “Mum Jobs” Solve? Public Problematization of Fertility Support, Work, and Care on RedNote</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
    <p><em>Full manuscript drafted</em></p>
    <p>This study examines how China’s “mum jobs” policy defines the problem of maternal employment and how RedNote users reinterpret and redefine these problems. Using Bacchi’s *What’s the Problem Represented to Be?* (WPR) approach, I analyze policy documents alongside more than 14,000 RedNote comments. I combine BERTopic-assisted topic modeling with qualitative thematic analysis to examine public discussions of work, caregiving, labor rights, and the gendered costs of motherhood. The study explores how public discourse extends, challenges, and redefines policy understandings of maternal employment and fertility support.</p>
     <p>
      <em>An earlier version of this research was presented at IAMCR 2026.</em>
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

<!-- Project 4: Mapping Male HPV Vaccination -->

<div class="paper-title">Mapping Knowledge Actors and Network Communities in Male HPV Vaccination Communication on Weibo</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
    <p><em>Manuscript in preparation</em></p>
    <p>In 2025, HPV vaccines were approved for male use in mainland China, bringing male vaccination into public debates over health risk, gender, access, and vaccine promotion. This ongoing project examines how male HPV vaccination communication is organized on Weibo. Using social network analysis, I map repost networks to identify network communities and structurally important actors. I then combine network analysis with qualitative analysis to examine who these actors are, what knowledge roles they perform, and what forms of vaccine knowledge they communicate.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<!-- Project 2: Male HPV Vaccination -->

<div class="paper-title">
 “Real Men Don’t Get the Shot?” Negotiating the Legitimacy of Male HPV Vaccination on Zhihu
</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
  <p><em>Manuscript in preparation</em></p>
  <p>
      This study examines how male HPV vaccination becomes a contested health practice in Chinese online discussions following the expansion of HPV vaccination to men in 2025. Drawing on Zhihu discussions and a psycho-discursive approach, I examine how users invoke risk, responsibility, sexual morality, and competing forms of knowledge to make vaccination appear legitimate or illegitimate and to construct different masculine health positions. The study explores how a historically feminized preventive practice acquires new gendered meanings when extended to men, and how public communication shapes what counts as rational, responsible, and legitimate health conduct.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<!-- Project 3: Male HPV Vaccination Advertising -->

<div class="paper-title">
 “Nine Out of Ten?” Evidence Evaluation and Persuasion Knowledge in Responses to a Male HPV Vaccine Advertisement on Zhihu
</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
  <p><em>Manuscript in preparation</em></p>
  <p>
      This study examines how Zhihu users respond to a male HPV vaccine advertisement claiming that “nine out of ten men may acquire HPV.” Drawing on quantified risk communication and the Persuasion Knowledge Model, I examine how users evaluate the statistical claim, infer the advertiser’s motives, and assess its persuasive tactics. Using theory-informed content analysis of more than 800 Zhihu answers, the project explores how evidence evaluation and persuasion knowledge become intertwined in naturally occurring public discussion.</p>
  </div>
</div>


<!-- Project 5: Just have a baby -->

<div class="paper-title">
“Just Have a Baby”: Reproductive Prescriptions and Medical Authority in Women’s Healthcare</div>
<div class="research-paper">
  <div class="research-content">
  <p><em>Manuscript in preparation</em></p>
  <p>
      This study examines reproductive prescriptions in women’s healthcare, such as advice to get married, find a partner, or have a baby in response to current health concerns. Drawing on the Theory of Communicative (Dis)enfranchisement, I examine how these messages connect women’s health concerns to expected intimate and reproductive life courses, and how medical authority shapes their interpretation. Using RedNote discussion threads, I analyze how users evaluate the medical legitimacy of such advice and how they reproduce, negotiate, or challenge the assumptions embedded in it.</p>
  </div>
</div>
